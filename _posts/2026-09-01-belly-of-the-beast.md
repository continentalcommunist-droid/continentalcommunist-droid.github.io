My time in Washington D.C. has meant many things for my intellectual development. I came here militantly naive and naively militant. That is, I understood my own limitations but saw them as a strength rather than a weakness. I grew up far from traditional centers of American political power, in the small beach town of Santa Barbara, California, which you may know from wealthy figures like Oprah who retire to the hills of Montecito just south of downtown State Street.

	Despite our town’s abundance of both self diagnosed Republicans and Democrats, and my own family’s inclination toward the progressive institutionalism of the latter, I found myself grappling with profound questions of self identity during the coronavirus pandemic of 2020 that led me to reach beyond the pale of my religious and political upbringing to more critical examinations of the powers that be, and I was fortunate to find a belief that a better world is possible beyond the self hating rhetoric of liberal determinism and conservative terrorism. Raised in a Christian church that would, after my departure, endorse the presidency of Donald Trump from the pulpit and invited a “rehabilitated and formerly gay” individual to speak at the youth group, I had developed a deep seated uneasiness with Protestant America, but also a profound sense of intrigue in the religio-political world through my family’s travels to the Holy Land in 2019. Baptized in the Jordan River, an experience which my atheism will never quite take from me, I saw both the power of belief and the terror of believers funded by the world’s great powers. For example, a taxi driver in Jerusalem casually called an Arab pedestrian a "cockroach", and there was a general unease I felt even as the region had not descended into the genocidal blood feud we’ve come to see as normal in 2026. 

	For a myriad of reasons not the least of which was the societal and academic upheaval of 2020, my high school years left me with more questions than answers. Although I began my journey into the discipline of Marxism during this period, I hesitate to attribute my university experience as simply an extension of this study. Although I sent my “early decision” application to The American University in Washington, D.C., the majority of my applications were sent to colleges up and down the East Coast in hopes that, if not politically, then in some cultural sense I could find respite from the chains I felt barred my intellectual consciousness in the Golden State. When I received my acceptance to American University, my excitement was certainly dictated by the feeling that I would have the opportunity to more adequately gauge the state of our nation, but was probably more directly a sense of relief and pride at having earned the invitation from a prestigious east coast school that my parents were more than happy to send me to following the turmoil of my high school years.

	Of course, I would be remiss if I didn’t briefly discuss the transition from theory to practice that occurred within this newfound freedom I experienced. My membership in the Democratic Socialists of America marked an important step from the radicalism of digital theory to the practice of mass organizing. Although I began paying dues to the group at the end of 2020 in my junior year of high school, it wasn’t until college that I became involved in person. In the Young Democratic Socialists of America chapter at American University, I was a member for my freshman and sophomore years, held the position of Social Media Director for two of the four semesters, and as a general member of the Steering Committee for the last of the four semesters. During this time, I tried to move our campus struggle beyond the revisionist message of America’s progressive electoralism through a series of graphics on topics ranging from the genocide in Palestine to a collaborative post with multiple Atlanta and Georgia chapters of the Young Democratic Socialists of America protesting the construction of “Cop City” (through our shared effort, the post received over 200 likes, a sizable accomplishment that put even AU’s Young Democrats club to shame). During my time as social media director, our Instagram account grew from 500 to 1000 followers, which again is a sizable accomplishment when compared to the followership of our College Democrat and College Republican clubs. 

	During my first two semesters of college, I was also an organizer with a liberal direct action organization called Climate Defiance, an experience that brought a sense of purpose without the integrity of a revolutionary theory. I also helped a good friend Raif Ahmed create an Instagram page called YourBossSucks, where we more acutely levelled a critical analysis of our university’s power structure and, taking inspiration from MorePerfectUnion (which Raif later would work at), took a stab at the synthesis of social media and left-wing reporting which is of utmost importance in our current struggle. The lack of theoretical coherence in many of the organizations I contributed my labor to led to the same place of contradiction within which all hitherto progressive struggles in America have perished. Driven by the focus on electoralism that became endemic to the Democratic Socialists of America, I spent a semester as a legislative intern for Santa Barbara’s Congressman Salud Carbajal. This was a life changing opportunity that I will forever be grateful for, but it gave an even greater acuteness to the contradiction I had selectively ignored as I maneuvered the halls of power in our nation’s capital: China is winning.
