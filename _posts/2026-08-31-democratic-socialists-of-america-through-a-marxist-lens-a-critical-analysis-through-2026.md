The most important fact for a rigorous Marxist assessment of the Democratic Socialists of America is that **the DSA of 2026 is not adequately described as simply a reformist pressure group seeking a more progressive Democratic Party**. Its official _Workers Deserve More_ program, drafted in 2026 after the 2025 National Convention, explicitly says DSA is “building a party,” identifies the antagonist as the global system of capital and the broader anti-democratic social structure, calls for a new constitution and a “democratic socialist republic,” proposes public ownership of the largest corporations and essential industries, and distinguishes reforms achievable under capitalism from the “complete victory” that would require a new society.  Its National Electoral Commission now declares an objective of an independent mass socialist party, opposition to ruling-class political forces, candidates who are “socialist organizers first, and legislators second,” cadre candidacies, and experimentation with independent ballot lines. 

That represents a substantial strategic movement from DSA's historical lineage. The organization formed in 1982 from the Democratic Socialist Organizing Committee and New American Movement; its own history presents Michael Harrington's DSOC strategy as a coalition among progressive trade unionists, civil-rights and feminist activists, and liberals in the McGovern wing of the Democratic Party. DSA historically embraced a pluralist, multi-tendency organization whose members could disagree about independent electoral work inside or outside the Democratic ballot line.  A Marxist critique that simply reproduces the picture of DSA from the Harrington era therefore misses real strategic changes since the Sanders-era expansion and, especially, the 2025–26 turn toward explicit party-building. 

**The central Marxist criticism in 2026 is consequently not that DSA lacks socialist language; it is that its program has moved faster than its organizational capacities, social implantation, and theory of political transition.** Marx and Engels define communists as the “most advanced and resolute section” of the workers' movement whose immediate aim includes the “formation of the proletariat into a class.”  DSA, by contrast, remains an open membership organization in which subscribing to its principles and paying dues are the basic formal requirements for membership in good standing.  DSA's own organizational research finds recruitment predominantly passive, individualized, and virtual rather than rooted in pre-existing groups of workers and neighbors; it also says increases in dues-paying membership do not automatically produce active members or “cadre organizers,” and that almost half of former members departed after a single year.  This is an unusually direct empirical confirmation of what, in Marxist organizational language, can be called a **mass–cadre conversion problem**.

On reform and revolution, DSA's present doctrine is considerably stronger than orthodox social-democratic gradualism. Marx's _Value, Price and Profit_ did not reject immediate economic struggles: he regarded them as necessary schools of resistance. His warning was that workers must understand they are “fighting with effects, but not with the causes” and turn their organized power into a lever for emancipation rather than confine themselves to recurring defensive battles.  DSA's 2026 formula—that some demands can be won now but their full realization requires a new society—can plausibly be read as an embryonic **transitional-program logic**.  The weakness is that DSA has not yet specified with comparable clarity _how_ struggles for rent control, health care, labor rights, public utilities, electoral reform, or socialist officeholders are supposed to accumulate into independent working-class governing capacity rather than a succession of reforms administered through capitalist institutions.

The sharpest theoretical contradiction concerns **the state**. Marx's lesson from the Paris Commune was that “the working class cannot simply lay hold of the ready-made state machinery” and use it unchanged.  Engels likewise described the state as a product of class antagonisms, while Marx's _Critique of the Gotha Programme_ defined genuine political freedom in terms of converting the state from an authority above society into one subordinate to it.  Lenin radicalized this into the requirement to break the bureaucratic-military apparatus and replace it with institutions of mass working-class democracy.  DSA has moved beyond simply defending the existing U.S. Constitution: abolishing the Senate and Electoral College and subordinating the executive and judiciary to a redesigned legislature would constitute a profound constitutional rupture. But its program remains largely **Congress-centered** and does not yet identify the institutions through which organized workers themselves would exercise continuous power over production and administration. 

A related economic weakness concerns **nationalization versus socialization**. DSA now proposes public ownership of the largest corporations and essential industries, as well as publicly owned energy, transit, hospitals, and housing.  This is much more structural than a conventional welfare-state platform. Yet Engels specifically warned that state ownership by itself leaves open the possibility that “The workers remain wage-workers — proletarians”; nationalization by an unchanged capitalist state does not automatically abolish the capitalist relation.  Marx's _Gotha_ critique similarly insists that consumption and income distribution follow from the “distribution of the conditions of production themselves.”  From that standpoint, DSA needs to specify not merely _who legally owns_ a nationalized enterprise, but who controls investment, production, workplace authority, surplus allocation, technological change, and planning.

DSA is substantially stronger from a Marxist standpoint on **race and anti-imperialism** than a class-reductionist criticism would suggest. Its program explicitly calls for finishing Reconstruction, reparations, minority-community investment, immigrant rights, Palestinian self-determination, withdrawal from foreign wars and overseas bases, and the end of U.S. sanctions against Cuba, Venezuela, and Iran.  Du Bois's _Black Reconstruction_, prominently placed on the Continental Communist reading list, demonstrates why American proletarian unity cannot be inferred mechanically from a common wage relation: white workers historically received what he called a “public and psychological wage,” while the self-activity of enslaved Black workers became a decisive historical force through what he conceptualized as a general strike.  Lenin's theory of imperialism similarly directs analysis toward monopoly and finance capital rather than treating war principally as bad policy.  The corrective for DSA is therefore neither race-blind class politics nor generic anti-Americanism, but an independent, multiracial, internationalist class strategy linking racial domination and imperial coercion to concrete relations of production, property, finance, and state power.

The resulting judgment is mixed but fairly definite: **DSA has increasingly adopted a Marxist-compatible destination and some Marxist-compatible strategic concepts, but it does not yet possess an organizational form, class implantation, or transition theory adequate to its declared objective.** Its decisive test is no longer whether it can popularize social-democratic reforms. It is whether it can make elections, unions, tenant struggles, anti-racist movements, and anti-imperialist campaigns into mechanisms for producing an organized working class capable of acting independently of both capitalist parties and, ultimately, of subordinating political and economic administration to democratic producer power. That is the standard suggested jointly by Marx, Engels, Lenin, Du Bois, and Debs, even though those thinkers do not constitute a single seamless doctrine. 

## Method and theoretical baseline

The Continental Communist [reading list](https://www.continentalcommunist.com/reading-list/) should not itself be treated as a party canon. We explicitly state that it is a “working bibliography,” contains authors who disagree profoundly with one another, and warn that scientific socialism should be a method of inquiry capable of confronting empirical evidence, criticism, institutional complexity, technological change, and historical experience rather than a method for accumulating convenient quotations. We also specifically warn that foreign socialist experiences should be studied historically rather than turned mechanically into templates for American politics. 

That methodological qualification is essential. The analysis below is therefore an **internal Marxist audit**, not an assertion that every item on the Continental list or every Marxist writer yields one mandatory organizational formula. The list itself prioritizes the _Communist Manifesto_, Engels's _Socialism: Utopian and Scientific_, Marx's _Value, Price and Profit_, Du Bois's _Black Reconstruction_, Debs's writings, _Critique of the Gotha Programme_, _The Eighteenth Brumaire_, and _Capital_; it separately includes Lenin's _Imperialism_ and _State and Revolution_.  I use Lenin's _What Is To Be Done?_ as a canonical supplement specifically for the question of vanguardism; it is **not** represented here as being on the Continental Communist list.

The principal theoretical tests are these. First, **historical materialism** asks whether DSA explains politics through changing relations of production, class formation, institutions, and the balance of social forces rather than predominantly through moral categories or personalities. Marx's _Eighteenth Brumaire_ is particularly useful here because it shows both that “state power is not suspended in the air” and that common material circumstances do not by themselves constitute a politically effective class: political organization matters. 

Second, **class struggle** requires distinguishing a working-class political project from generic “people versus elites” populism. The _Manifesto_ makes the communist function both universalizing and organizational: communists articulate the common interests of the proletariat and understand the broader direction of the struggle.  This does not mean Marx and Engels required communists to form a tiny sect outside every broader workers' organization. On the contrary, the same passage says communists do not constitute a separate party opposed to other working-class parties. The relevant criterion is whether socialists operate as a coherent, strategically advanced current _within the real class movement_. 

Third, **reform versus revolution** is tested not by whether an organization supports reforms—the classical Marxist tradition plainly did—but by the relationship between immediate struggles and transformation of the wage relation and political power. Marx argued that unions are indispensable centers of resistance but fail when they reduce themselves to a permanent “guerilla war” against capitalism's effects rather than using accumulated organization for the abolition of the wage system. 

Fourth, **state theory** asks whether democracy is conceptualized merely as gaining a parliamentary majority or as transforming the institutions through which one class rules another. Engels located the state's origin in irreconcilable social antagonisms, Marx regarded the Commune as evidence that the old machinery could not simply be appropriated, and Lenin argued for replacement of bureaucratic administration with recallable, directly accountable institutions. 

Fifth, **organization and vanguardism** require separating Marx's broad conception of an advanced communist current from Lenin's historically specific model of an organization of professional revolutionaries under Russian autocracy. Lenin's formulation—an “organization that will consist of professional revolutionaries”—cannot reasonably be transplanted literally into a legal mass organization in the contemporary United States.  But his functional problem remains pertinent: how does a socialist organization transform episodic or spontaneous struggles into durable political consciousness, leadership, and organization rather than simply following the spontaneous rhythms of trade-union, electoral, or protest activity? 

Finally, Du Bois and Lenin extend a crude two-class model. Du Bois demonstrates that racial status and state-enforced hierarchy can prevent workers with objectively overlapping economic interests from forming a common political subject; Lenin situates national capitalism inside a world system of monopolies, finance capital, capital export, and interstate competition.  These are indispensable for assessing a socialist organization operating in the historically racialized center of the global capitalist system.

## Organization, vanguardism, and class composition

DSA combines unusually open mass membership with a representative democratic national structure. Its constitution makes membership available to anyone subscribing to its principles and defines good standing through the dues system. The National Convention is formally the highest decision-making body, is held at least biennially, and elects through delegates the National Political Committee; the current NPC has twenty-five members, establishes major political and organizational direction between conventions, directs staff, and elects a seven-person Steering Committee.  Most organizing is nevertheless experienced locally: DSA's internal research says the vast majority of organizing happens at the chapter level and that chapter experiences vary dramatically. 

This design has a genuine Marxist virtue. The _Manifesto_ does **not** make communist effectiveness synonymous with organizational exclusivity. Its communist current is non-sectarian in relation to the broader workers' movement while simultaneously functioning as its theoretically and practically advanced pole.  DSA's multi-tendency character therefore cannot be refuted merely by invoking Marx. Its openness makes it possible to incorporate workers politicized by elections, strikes, racial-justice struggles, tenant organizing, antiwar activity, or other struggles without requiring prior mastery of an ideological catechism. DSA's own history explicitly presents pluralism and multi-tendency organization as core attributes that facilitated its post-2016 expansion. 

The difficulty is the second half of the Marx–Engels formula: being the _advanced_ element presupposes a process capable of creating people who can synthesize experiences across struggles, reproduce organizing knowledge, and maintain strategic continuity during political downturns. DSA's constitutional membership threshold does not itself require political education, organizing activity, workplace or tenant implantation, or demonstrated competency.  That is perfectly defensible for a **mass membership** layer. It becomes problematic when mass membership is treated as equivalent to organized socialist capacity.

DSA's own empirical work makes this distinction explicitly. Research published by _Democratic Left_ in 2026 says recruitment has been predominantly passive, individual, and virtual rather than organized recruitment of groups with existing relationships such as unionists, neighborhood groups, or diaspora networks. It further states that dues-paying growth does not imply that members become active or develop into “DSA cadre organizers.”  Almost half of former members in the underlying cohort analysis left after one year, while retention rates were reported as basically unchanged since 2020. Interviews pointed toward one-on-ones, mentoring, political education, social integration, and chapter development as mechanisms associated with stronger retention. 

This is strikingly close to Lenin's functional concern about spontaneity. _What Is To Be Done?_ argued that an organization whose political life follows spontaneous economic struggle will reproduce the limitations of that struggle; hence Lenin's insistence on politically trained organizers capable of integrating multiple fronts into a general political project.  One need not accept Lenin's clandestine organization or every claim about consciousness to recognize the problem. **An organization whose recruitment rises sharply after external political shocks, but which cannot consistently turn those recruits into durable organizers, is being reproduced partly by the political environment rather than reproducing itself through organized class relationships.**

DSA's recent membership history strongly supports that diagnosis. Internal organizational research describes successive 2016–20 growth waves triggered largely by external events; monthly joins increased dramatically after Donald Trump's 2016 election, while later surges were associated with Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez's 2018 victory, the 2020 Sanders campaign, the pandemic, the George Floyd uprising, and DSA's membership drive. Membership in good standing peaked just under 79,000 in 2021, declined to about 51,000 by October 2024, and entered another rapid-growth period after November 2024.  This is not evidence that mass recruitment is undesirable. It is evidence that DSA's political reproduction has historically been highly **event-elastic**.

A classical Leninist response would be to build a distinct cadre organization. A more defensible synthesis of Marx, Lenin, and DSA's democratic commitments would instead create **differentiated layers of participation**: an extremely open mass membership combined with a clearly defined, democratically accountable organizer layer whose members undertake systematic political education, sustained organizing assignments, leadership development, and responsibility for reproducing new organizers. DSA has already moved partly in this direction: its own internal report uses the vocabulary of cadre development, and its 2025 electoral policy explicitly prioritizes “cadre candidates.”  The Marxist criticism is that this logic should not be confined chiefly to candidates for public office.

There is also a problem of **national coherence versus local autonomy**. Chapter-centered organizing allows experimentation and adjustment to local material conditions; DSA's own research notes substantial differences between large urban chapters and smaller rural or suburban organizations.  Yet a national organization seeking to become a party must be able to coordinate labor strategy, elections, political education, national campaigns, discipline of elected representatives, and responses to crises on a more continuous basis than a loose federation of local practices. The 2025 enlargement of the NPC to twenty-five members and strengthening of national electoral standards are moves toward such coordination.  The unresolved Marxist question is whether DSA can create **unity in action after democratic decision** without destroying the pluralism that made mass expansion possible.

On **class composition**, rigor requires rejecting an often-repeated but poorly substantiated polemic: the available contemporary public evidence is not sufficient to declare DSA simply a “petty-bourgeois” or professional-managerial organization. DSA's public organizational research contains much better membership and retention information than occupational class data. A 2024–25 membership survey discussed by an NPC member found 66 percent of respondents identified as white and 34 percent with other racial or ethnic categories, but the author also notes limitations in DSA's demographic information and _Democratic Left_ explicitly states that signed articles do not necessarily express the organization's official position.  Those figures cannot substitute for an occupational class analysis.

For an organization proclaiming itself the nucleus of a working-class party, however, **not having a publicly developed, high-quality class map is itself a strategic deficiency**. Marx's _Brumaire_ distinguishes people who merely occupy similar circumstances from a class capable of asserting a common political interest: organization turns objective location into political agency.  DSA should therefore know not simply the age, race, gender, or dues status of its members, but their industries, occupations, employment relations, union status, workplace concentration, housing tenure, debt relations, supervisory authority, and location in strategically important supply chains. Without this, “working class” risks functioning as an ideological identity rather than an empirically mapped social force.

## Reform, revolution, electoralism, and the state

DSA's 2026 program deserves to be read literally before it is criticized. It says the organization is building a party for a democratic society of the working class, treats the system of capital rather than individual bad actors as the adversary, seeks a new constitution and democratic socialist republic, and explicitly states that reforms won under existing conditions are not identical to complete socialist victory.  That is a major difference from a program which assumes capitalism can simply be regulated indefinitely into socialism.

In this respect, DSA is actually compatible with an important point frequently erased in mechanical reform-versus-revolution debates. Marx does not tell workers to reject partial improvements. In _Value, Price and Profit_, he regards wage struggles as necessary resistance and argues that surrender in everyday conflict would leave workers incapable of larger struggles. His objection is to becoming “exclusively absorbed” in those struggles and failing to transform accumulated organization into a movement against the wage system itself. 

This produces a useful test for every DSA reform. Medicare for All, social housing, shorter working time, free education, public transit, union rights, rent control, public hospitals, and stronger social provision can have at least **three different political functions**. They can be ends in themselves within a stable capitalist order; they can be electoral promises used to assemble a progressive voting coalition; or they can be demands whose campaigns increase organization, reduce workers' dependence on employers and markets, expose capitalist veto power, and generate institutions capable of further struggle. DSA's own 2026 statement that demands should be pushed to their “furthest extent” points toward the third interpretation, but an explicit operational theory connecting each reform to an increase in class power remains underdeveloped. 

**Electoralism presents the same ambiguity.** DSA's historical tradition was consciously coalitionist. Its own institutional history describes DSOC's major contribution as constructing a coalition between progressive unionists, social movements, and liberals in the Democratic Party and states that the merged DSA worked in coalitions with non-socialist progressives both inside and outside Democratic politics.  Such a strategy can win reforms and reach millions, but from a Marxist standpoint it also produces a structural danger: the socialist organization can become the mobilizing left flank of a political coalition whose officeholders, funding structures, institutional incentives, and governing horizons remain capitalist.

The contemporary DSA is trying to correct that problem. The 2025 Convention mandated independent ballot-line experimentation, more cadre candidates, tighter national endorsements, and a socialist-in-office network.  Nationally endorsed candidates are now expected to have prior DSA leadership experience, uphold the national program, identify publicly as socialists, recruit into DSA, develop DSA leaders through their campaigns, and caucus with socialist elected officials. Endorsement decisions must also evaluate political independence, party-building, the Democratic Party, and the transition to socialism.  The NEC goes further by declaring the goal of an independent mass socialist party and saying DSA does not seek a lesser-evil governing coalition with ruling-class political forces. 

That is not a cosmetic change. It converts the Marxist criticism from **“DSA has no concept of political independence”** into the subtler question **“Can DSA operationalize the political independence it now officially professes?”** The answer remains unsettled because electoral institutions generate powerful pressures toward candidate autonomy, professional campaign staffs, fundraising imperatives, legislative compromise, constituency service, media personalization, and coalition-making. The Marxist criterion should therefore be organizational: an election is strategically successful when the campaign leaves behind _more independent socialist organization than existed before it_, even when the candidate loses; an election can be strategically regressive when office is won but the socialist organization becomes subordinate to the officeholder.

DSA itself provides an example of the scale elections can achieve. Its National Political Committee described Zohran Mamdani's November 2025 New York City mayoral victory as a historic socialist electoral success, reporting that thousands of DSA members participated among a campaign mobilizing more than 100,000 volunteers and emphasizing that the post-election task was to build a mass working-class movement capable of winning the program against opposition from concentrated wealth.  From a Marxist perspective, however, the decisive evidence will not be the symbolic magnitude of the victory but whether electoral mobilization is converted into durable workplace, tenant, neighborhood, and party organization able to exert power independently of an administration.

The deeper problem appears at the level of the **state**. Marx's _Civil War in France_ is categorical that the working class cannot simply inherit the existing machinery. The Commune's importance for him was that its representatives were responsible and recallable and that it was conceived as a working rather than merely parliamentary body.  Engels's _Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State_ similarly treats the state not as an ideologically neutral instrument but as a power generated by class antagonisms and increasingly separated from society. 

Marx's _Critique of the Gotha Programme_ sharpens the point: “Freedom consists in converting the state from an organ superimposed upon society into one completely subordinate to it.”  Lenin interprets the Commune as requiring the replacement of bureaucracy by democratically accountable administration: election and recall, the elimination of privileged official salaries, and mass participation in administrative functions.  Whether one accepts Lenin's broader revolutionary conclusions or not, these passages provide a clear Marxist test: **socialism is not merely a different policy content flowing through the old state form**.

DSA's current program should receive partial credit on precisely this question. It no longer proposes simply winning offices under an unchanged constitutional framework. It seeks abolition of the Senate and Electoral College, proportional representation, a multi-party system, a larger House, and replacement of the presidency and Supreme Court with institutions subordinate to Congress.  Relative to ordinary American progressivism, this is an exceptionally radical constitutional program.

Yet it remains incomplete from the Marx–Commune perspective. Congress is still treated as the principal locus of national sovereignty; the program does not specify a system of worker, workplace, tenant, or communal institutions that would continuously exercise democratic economic and administrative power.  It is therefore possible to imagine fulfilling much of the constitutional plank while retaining a professional legislative class, a bureaucratic administrative apparatus, hierarchical workplaces, wage labor, and a separation between citizens as voters and producers as subjects of managerial authority. A Marxist transformation of the state would require eliminating that separation progressively, not only making representative institutions more democratic.

This becomes especially important in DSA's position on police and prisons. Its 2026 program correctly recognizes coercive institutions as structurally connected to class power, calling for demilitarization and steps toward abolition of the existing police and prison system.  But classical Marxist state theory poses the next question immediately: **what democratic institutions perform legitimate collective administrative and protective functions in the new order, and to whom are they accountable?** A theory of dismantling coercive hierarchy must therefore be accompanied by a positive theory of democratic social administration.

Finally, public ownership does not resolve the state question automatically. Engels warned in _Socialism: Utopian and Scientific_ that converting enterprises into state property does not by itself abolish their capitalist character.  If employees continue receiving wages from a hierarchical public corporation while investment and production are directed by a bureaucracy beyond their effective control, the legal title has changed but the producers' subordinate relation to the process of production may persist. DSA's program says public ownership will ensure democratic control, but it does not yet specify the institutional architecture of that control. 

The Marxist criticism of DSA's state strategy, therefore, is **not** that it merely seeks minor parliamentary reform. As of 2026 that would be inaccurate. The sharper criticism is that it has outlined a democratic constitutional rupture without yet developing a sufficiently material theory of **working-class rule after the rupture**.

## Program, race, imperialism, alliances, and ideology

DSA's economic program contains an unresolved tension between **redistribution** and **transformation of production**. Its major immediate demands include universal health care, public hospitals, social housing, rent control, shorter working time, free education, public infrastructure, wealth taxation, and labor rights. It also goes beyond redistribution by proposing public ownership of the largest corporations and essential industries. 

Marx's _Critique of the Gotha Programme_ is unusually relevant here because Marx criticizes socialist programs that concentrate on “fair distribution” without transforming the underlying distribution of productive conditions. His argument is that consumption patterns are consequences of the mode of production: once the material conditions of production remain monopolized by a property-owning class, unequal distribution continually reproduces itself. 

Applied to DSA, this does **not** imply that health care, housing, education, or transfers are irrelevant. Such programs can reduce workers' dependence on employers, increase social reproduction outside commodity markets, and improve organizing capacity. The critique is that these gains become Marxistically transitional only when connected to a transfer of control over the **production and allocation of social surplus**. Public health care without socialized pharmaceutical production and investment, public housing without a strategy against land and financial capital, or public utilities without producer and consumer control can remain islands of public provision inside an economy still dominated by capital accumulation.

DSA's public-ownership plank begins to address this, but Engels exposes the residual problem: state ownership is not identical with the associated producers taking control.  A developed socialist economic program would consequently have to specify governance structures for publicly owned enterprises, mechanisms of democratic planning, worker representation, consumer and community participation, investment allocation, technological strategy, ecological accounting, and the disposition of economic surpluses. Otherwise “public” can mean ownership by an administrative state rather than actual social ownership.

DSA's **class language** also requires refinement. Its 2026 program has improved on purely moral populism by explicitly identifying private ownership, profit, capital, bosses, and class society. It says the problem extends beyond individual bosses to the system of capital itself.  Nonetheless, its frequent rhetorical opposition between the “billionaire class” and working people compresses a much more complex class structure. Marx's _Eighteenth Brumaire_ is useful precisely because it analyzes different fractions of property owners, peasants, political representatives, bureaucrats, and social groupings rather than reducing politics to the poorest versus the richest. 

A serious contemporary materialist strategy would distinguish finance capital, industrial capital, technology platforms, landlords and real-estate capital, small proprietors, senior managerial strata, professional strata with contradictory class locations, union bureaucracies, precarious wage workers, organized workers in strategic industries, public-sector workers, undocumented labor, and different fractions of the self-employed. That need not produce sectarian taxonomy for its own sake. Its practical purpose is to identify which groups possess structural power, which can become stable allies, which oscillate politically, where capital's veto points lie, and which worker concentrations can disrupt accumulation.

Here the Continental Communist reading list's methodological warning is important. Its stated objective is not quote accumulation but a scientific socialism capable of engaging actual institutional complexity.  DSA's 2026 program is understandably an agitational document rather than an economic monograph; the proper corrective is therefore not to fill the platform with theoretical jargon, but to support it with a permanent **materialist research and strategic-analysis function**.

### Race and reconstruction within class formation

Du Bois's _Black Reconstruction_ makes a major contribution that any American Marxism ignoring race would fail to assimilate. The book's structure begins by distinguishing the Black worker, white worker, and planter and contains an entire chapter titled “The General Strike,” treating enslaved people's withdrawal of labor and movement toward Union lines as active historical agency rather than passive emancipation from above.  Du Bois's subsequent analysis of a “public and psychological wage” helps explain why objective exploitation does not automatically yield interracial class unity: relative racial status and differential access to civic institutions can bind sections of workers politically to a racial order from which ruling groups benefit. 

This is a strong argument **against** any Marxist critique that dismisses DSA's anti-racism as a diversion from class. DSA's 2026 program calls for “Finish Reconstruction,” reparations, treaty and tribal sovereignty, anti-discrimination enforcement, and major investment in historically neglected communities.  In Du Boisian terms, attacking the material and civic structures reproducing racial hierarchy can be a precondition for, rather than an alternative to, proletarian unity.

But Du Bois also provides grounds for criticizing liberal identity politics. His analysis is not mainly an appeal for better representation among elites; it concerns labor, property, political power, racialized state institutions, and the failure to create a durable coalition of Black and white producers. The Marxist–Du Boisian standard for DSA should consequently be **multiracial class formation**, measured by whether anti-racist politics creates stronger common organizations among workers while directly attacking material racial differentials.

DSA's own evidence indicates unfinished work. A 2024–25 membership survey discussed in its publication _Democratic Left_ found 66 percent of respondents identified as white; the author acknowledges chapters whose membership does not correspond closely to the demographics of their cities.  Because this was a voluntary survey and the article is signed commentary rather than official organizational doctrine, it should not be overinterpreted. But it reinforces the case for systematic recruitment through workplaces, unions, tenant organizations, neighborhoods, immigrant networks, and other existing collective relationships rather than expecting a multiracial working-class organization to emerge automatically from online individual membership.

### Imperialism and internationalism

DSA's formal international platform is among the areas where it most clearly departs from mainstream U.S. social democracy. It calls for ending foreign wars and overseas bases, cutting aid to Israel, Palestinian self-determination and right of return, ending economic sanctions against Cuba, Venezuela, and Iran, and broad immigrant rights. 

Lenin's _Imperialism_ offers both support and a corrective. His famous definition centers on “dominance of monopolies and finance capital,” the increasing role of capital export, international monopolist associations, and territorial division among major powers.  His discussion of finance capital emphasizes concentrated financial power and the revenues extracted by financial oligarchies.  The implication is that anti-imperialism cannot be reduced to pacifism or a moral objection to bad foreign-policy decisions. It requires locating military and diplomatic policy inside the accumulation strategies of monopoly capital, financial institutions, geopolitical competition, supply chains, energy systems, and state power.

Debs expresses the same class principle more directly in the 1918 Canton speech: “The master class has always declared the wars; the subject class has always fought the battles.”  DSA's “working-class foreign policy” framing is consequently close to a Debsian tradition. 

There is nevertheless a potential weakness in describing sanctions chiefly as punishment of governments that act “independently of the United States.”  A Marxist internationalism should not define socialist alignment by whether a foreign state conflicts with Washington. Opposition to U.S. imperial domination does not turn the ruling class or state apparatus of every U.S. geopolitical opponent into an agent of proletarian emancipation. Marx's _Gotha_ critique emphasizes international working-class struggle rather than an abstract “brotherhood of peoples” mediated by governments.  The corrective is **independent proletarian internationalism**: oppose U.S. military, financial, and sanctions power while simultaneously maintaining political independence from capitalist or bureaucratic ruling groups elsewhere.

### Alliances without class subordination

DSA's coalition tradition is not inherently anti-Marxist. Marx himself criticized the Gotha program's Lassallean characterization of every non-proletarian class as one undifferentiated reactionary mass, observing that different intermediate strata can move politically under changing historical conditions.  Lenin's account of a “people's revolution” similarly discusses working-class alliances with other oppressed producing classes rather than a numerically isolated proletariat acting alone. 

The Marxist issue is therefore **which class leads the alliance and on what program**. DSA's historical coalition with trade-union officials, social movements, and liberal Democrats became problematic to the extent that socialist goals were subordinated to maintaining a progressive coalition whose institutional center remained the Democratic Party. DSA's own history documents the Harrington-era centrality of this approach. 

The current NEC formulation—an independent socialist party opposing ruling-class political forces rather than seeking a lesser-evil governing coalition—constitutes an explicit attempt to establish a different principle.  In Marxist terms, that is the correct direction: **maximum breadth in social alliances, maximum clarity about independent working-class political organization**. The unresolved question is whether this principle can survive the practical incentives generated by close elections, legislative bargaining, union leadership relationships, nonprofit coalitions, and popular progressive officeholders.

This timeline suggests that DSA's central historical contradiction has changed. **The old contradiction** was between socialist identity and a political strategy structurally centered on progressive coalitionism. **The new contradiction** is between an increasingly explicit strategy of independent socialist party-building and an organization whose membership formation, workplace implantation, cadre reproduction, economic program, and state-transition theory remain only partially adapted to that objective.

## Conclusion and corrective measures

The Marxist case against contemporary DSA is strongest when it avoids two easy caricatures. The first is that DSA is merely a liberal pressure organization whose horizon is Scandinavian welfare capitalism. Its 2026 program and 2025 electoral strategy no longer support that description: DSA officially advocates an independent socialist party, a different constitutional order, public ownership, class rule by working people, and a distinction between reforms under capitalism and socialist transformation.  The second caricature is that an authentically Marxist DSA would simply copy Bolshevik organizational forms from 1917. The Continental Communist reading list itself expressly warns against turning foreign historical experiences into American templates. 

The more rigorous conclusion is that **DSA has entered a transitional organizational phase in which its principal task is to make its material organization catch up with its declared program**.

**Turn the 2026 platform into an explicit transition strategy.** Every major demand should answer a second question beyond “would this improve workers' lives?”: _what new organization, capacities, rights, institutions, or control over resources would this victory place in workers' hands?_ Marx's distinction in _Value, Price and Profit_ between necessary immediate struggles and the abolition of the wage system supplies the criterion.  A health-care campaign should build durable worker and patient organization; a housing campaign should build tenant power; a public-energy campaign should build institutions for worker and community governance; a shorter-workweek campaign should increase workers' autonomous time and organizational capacity. The strategic chain should be explicit: **reform → organization → expanded class capacity → further confrontation with capital → democratization of production and state power**.

**Institutionalize a mass-membership/cadre-organizer distinction without creating an undemocratic elite.** DSA's openness should remain. Marx and Engels's anti-sectarian formulation supports a broad relationship to the workers' movement.  But DSA's own evidence demonstrates that dues membership and organized capacity are not the same thing.  A defined organizer layer should undergo political education, demonstrate practical organizing competence, mentor new members, accept sustained assignments, and be held democratically accountable. Leadership positions and nationally endorsed candidacies should increasingly be drawn from that reproduced organizer layer. This captures the functional strength of Lenin's cadre insight without mechanically importing a clandestine Russian model into twenty-first-century American conditions. 

**Shift recruitment from individual affiliation toward collective social implantation.** DSA's own finding that recruitment is disproportionately passive, individual, and virtual is strategically more important than the raw size of its membership rolls.  Recruitment priorities should increasingly involve groups of coworkers, union reformers, tenants in the same buildings, logistics workers, teachers, nurses, public employees, manufacturing workers, service workers, immigrant networks, and other people already bound by durable social relations. The goal is not statistical worker authenticity; it is to transform DSA chapters from aggregates of sympathetic individuals into **networks embedded in the places where capitalist society is reproduced**.

**Create a serious national class-composition and political-economy research capacity.** Historical materialism requires more than calling the enemy “billionaires.” The _Brumaire_ demonstrates the importance of concrete class fractions and political representation; Lenin's _Imperialism_ does the same for finance and monopoly capital.  DSA should continuously map member occupations, industries, unions, workplace concentrations, housing relations, corporate ownership networks, logistics bottlenecks, financial institutions, regional economies, and capital-state linkages. Such analysis should guide where organizers are concentrated and which campaigns have the greatest capacity to alter the balance of class forces.

**Make public ownership inseparable from producer and popular control.** Engels's warning about state ownership is decisive: changing the legal proprietor does not automatically abolish the wage relation.  DSA's public-ownership program should therefore specify representative structures for workers and affected communities, transparent social planning, democratic investment allocation, limits on managerial hierarchy, mechanisms of recall and accountability, and control of technological decisions by those affected.  “Public” must come to mean **socially governed**, not merely federally incorporated.

**Complete the theory of the democratic socialist republic.** DSA's call for a new constitution is arguably the most strategically consequential element of its 2026 program, because it acknowledges that socialist power cannot simply mean electing better personnel to unchanged U.S. institutions.  Yet Marx's Commune writings push further: political representatives should be subordinated to active society rather than becoming a separate governing stratum.  DSA should develop a concrete, democratic account of how workplace organizations, unions, tenant institutions, local assemblies, and other organs of popular self-government relate to a transformed legislature and public administration. The goal should be _less separation between rulers and ruled_, not merely more representative rulers.

**Treat elections as instruments of party-building rather than the party as an instrument of elections.** DSA's 2025 endorsement criteria already make this reversal explicit by demanding that candidates recruit into DSA, develop leaders, uphold a shared program, and regard themselves as socialist organizers before legislators.  That standard should become enforceable and measurable. The key post-election questions should be: How many durable organizers were created? Did chapter membership become more active? Were workplace and tenant structures strengthened? Did the candidate remain accountable to collective organization? Was political independence increased? The emerging independent-ballot strategy should be expanded as material capacity allows, without turning ballot purity into a substitute for class power. 

**Adopt Du Bois rather than either class reductionism or liberal identity politics.** The American working class is historically produced through racialized labor markets, citizenship regimes, property relations, policing, and differential social status. Du Bois's analysis shows why racial hierarchy can materially obstruct class formation.  DSA's Reconstruction and reparations planks are therefore compatible with rather than external to Marxist class politics.  The organizational task is to make struggles against racial domination vehicles for common working-class organization while directly attacking inequalities that make abstract appeals to unity materially hollow.

**Convert anti-imperialism into class internationalism.** DSA is correct, from a Leninist and Debsian standpoint, to reject war, military bases, sanctions, and U.S. domination.  But its internationalism should be organized around workers and oppressed peoples rather than geopolitical camps. Opposition to Washington should coexist with complete independence from foreign capitalist, oligarchic, or bureaucratic ruling classes. International labor coordination, organizing across multinational supply chains, solidarity with independent unions and socialist organizations, and analysis of U.S. finance capital would give the anti-imperialist program a more specifically Marxist content.

Taken together, these measures point toward a model that is **neither Harringtonite coalition social democracy nor a reenactment of early-twentieth-century Bolshevism**. It would be a democratic mass organization with an organized cadre layer; a party willing to contest elections without being constituted by electoral institutions; a movement rooted in workplaces and communities rather than predominantly in individualized political affiliation; an anti-racist organization whose unit of analysis remains material class formation; an anti-imperialist movement independent of competing ruling classes; and a socialist program in which public ownership culminates in democratic social control over production.

In that sense, the most consequential sentence in DSA's new program may be its declaration that the organization is **building a party**.  Under a Marxist standard, however, a party is not created primarily by naming it. It comes into existence when a politically conscious organization becomes capable of linking otherwise fragmented struggles, reproducing durable leadership, forming workers into an independent political subject, and converting economic and political contests into an accumulating struggle over who governs production and society. That was the organizational content of Marx and Engels's “formation of the proletariat into a class”; it is also the standard by which DSA's post-2025 experiment should ultimately be judged.
